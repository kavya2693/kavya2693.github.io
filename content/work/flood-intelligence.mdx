<S n="01" t="Problem">

Cities need to know where water will collect — and what to do about it — before the storm, not after. The natural first model is a regression: **rainfall in, flooded area out**, fitted across the world's worst-affected regions and applied to Gulf cities. That requires both numbers, sourced, for the same flood.

</S>

<S n="02" t="Context">

- **Scope:** twelve flood regions — ten global hotspots and two in the UAE — over 2000–2025.
- **Users the system is ultimately for:** city operators who decide where pumps, tankers and road closures go.
- **Standard:** every figure must be auditable by a third party. A risk model built on numbers nobody can trace is not something an authority can act on.

</S>

<S n="03" t="Data">

48 documented flood events. For each, the dataset needs peak 24-hour rainfall and inundated extent, each with a resolvable source, an evidence tier and a measurement method. Dam- and levee-failure events are excluded (rainfall doesn't drive them), as are extents reported for whole administrative areas.

| Field | Events with a sourced value |
|---|---|
| Peak 24 h rainfall | 12 of 48 (25%) |
| Inundated extent | 14 of 48 (29%) |
| **Both** | **2 of 48 (4.2%)** |

</S>

<S n="06" t="Modeling / AI approach">

The model that was planned is a leave-one-site-out regression of inundated extent on rainfall. It is implemented, and it **refuses to fit** when it has too few paired observations — raising `NotEstimable` instead of returning a confident line through two points.

With the regression ruled out, the analysis turned to *why* the data are missing: a coverage audit by site and by flood type, and a rank correlation between rainfall coverage and extent coverage across sites.

</S>

<S n="07" t="Evaluation">

- **57 tests**, including the validator against broken fixtures.
- Rainfall coverage and extent coverage are **anti-correlated across sites: Spearman ρ = −0.76, p = 0.0042, n = 12.**
- The mechanism: met agencies gauge rain where cities are; satellite mapping activates where floods are wide, flat and rural. Urban sites (Mumbai, Jakarta) have rainfall and no extent; riverine sites (Lokoja, the Yangtze) have the reverse. The pattern holds within a site too.
- Only 3 of the 12 sourced rainfall figures are top-tier evidence.

</S>

<S n="08" t="Production considerations">

For an urban twin in a flat Gulf city, two things decide feasibility before any model does:

- **Elevation data.** Across a test area, global DEMs report relief several times larger than the true relief of the city — the error is as large as the hydraulic signal. A survey-grade DEM is a prerequisite, not an upgrade.
- **The handover format.** Building to the city authority's own stormwater design standard makes handover a format match rather than a migration.
- **The decision is drawdown.** Operators care when water clears and what speeds it up — not just peak depth — so the twin must be time-resolved.

</S>

<S n="09" t="Results">

- A public, provenance-validated dataset of 48 events across 12 regions.
- A finding that changes what to build: **the bottleneck in flood ML is paired measurement, not model choice.**
- The only published inundated area for any UAE event is **23.8 km²** (Dubai, April 2024). Nothing is published for any Fujairah event.

</S>

<S n="10" t="Tradeoffs / lessons">

- **Let the data veto the plan.** A regression on two points would have produced a chart and a false conclusion.
- **Write the validator before the dataset.** It turned "I think this is sourced" into a test that fails.
- **Next step:** reproduce the Dubai 23.8 km² figure independently from Sentinel-1 radar — the first paired observation the Gulf has.

<Note>A well-evidenced negative result is worth more than a regression on two points.</Note>

</S>
